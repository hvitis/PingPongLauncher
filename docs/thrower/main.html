How it works

Six subsystems, each simple on its own. Everything you print is in PETG. Everything electrical comes from a hobby-electronics shop. The rest is bolts, two skate bearings and two rubber-tyred wheels.

1 · Power

Four AA cells in a closed holder on the left wall give 4.8–6.4 V. The POWER rocker switch on the back panel feeds that straight to the motor driver and both servos, which are all rated for it. The Pico 2 W tolerates at most 5.5 V on VSYS, so it gets the battery through two 1N4001 diodes in series. They drop about 1.4 V, which keeps fresh alkalines (6.4 V) safe, and the Pico still runs when the pack sags to 4 V. A 470 µF capacitor at the driver absorbs motor start-up spikes. The firmware also ramps the motors up gently so the cells don't brown out the controller. Expect 2–4 hours of play per set of NiMH cells.

2 · Throwing: two spinning wheels

Two N20 gearmotors (6 V, 5:1, about 3000 rpm) each spin a 65 mm yellow TT-motor wheel with a rubber tyre. A printed hub clamps the 3 mm D-shaft with an M3 set screw and carries a stub shaped like the TT motor shaft the wheel was made for (or a LEGO cross, for LEGO wheels). One wheel sits above the ball and one below. The gap between them is the ball diameter minus ~2 mm (ball_squeeze), so the soft tyres grip the 40 mm ball and fling it out at roughly the wheels' surface speed. A 65 mm wheel at 3000 rpm has a rim speed of 10.2 m/s, so balls leave at about 2.5–8 m/s, which is the range of normal rallies. The top motor sits in vertical slots. Loosen two screws to adjust the squeeze or to fit a worn tyre.

The original TT 1:48 motors only turn ~200 rpm, which gives a 0.6 m/s dribble. That is why the design uses N20s.

3 · Speed and spin

The DRV8833 driver gives each motor its own 20 kHz PWM, so the wheels can run at different speeds. SPEED sets the average duty (30–100 %). SPIN splits it: turn right and the top wheel runs faster, which gives topspin (the ball dips). Turn left for backspin (the ball floats). A dead band in the middle gives a flat ball. The Simulate tab shows the Magnus effect on the trajectory.

4 · Ball feed: the escapement

Balls drop from your basket through the hopper funnel into a vertical feed tube that holds a stack of balls. A rocker on the second micro-servo pokes two fingers through windows in the tube, 30 mm apart. In the rest position the lower finger is in and holds the stack. To release, the rocker tips the other way. The upper finger slides in under ball 2 and lifts the stack a few millimetres off ball 1, then the lower finger pulls out and ball 1 drops alone. When the rocker tips back, the column settles one ball lower. The RATE knob sets one ball every 5 s down to every 0.8 s.

5 · From tube to wheels

The released ball falls into the launcher head. It drops a few millimetres, then rolls around a curved floor (the elbow, 48 mm radius) that turns it 90° toward the front. A short straight run delivers it into the pinch between the wheels. The entry of the head sits exactly on the head's tilt pivot, so changing the elevation never misaligns the head with the fixed tube above it.

6 · Aim: swing and elevation

Left/right (swing): the whole turret (head, tube, hopper) turns on an M8 bolt running in two 608 skate bearings in the base lid. The swing servo (MG90S) under the bearings never carries weight. Its horn is screwed to a printed coupler whose hex socket slides over the bolt's nyloc nut and transmits only torque. Range ±35°. The SWING button starts or stops it. A long press cycles the patterns: sweep (continuous), left · centre · right (one position per ball) and random.

Up/down (elevation): the head tilts between the yoke cheeks on two M5×12 bolts. They screw through the cheeks, and their plain tips turn in bores in the head. An M4 bolt with a printed thumb knob runs in an arc slot and locks it anywhere from −5° to +30°. Ticks every 5° are moulded into the cheek, with long ticks at 0°, 15° and 30°.

7 · Controls and phone

Back panel: POWER switch, MOTORS / SWING / FEED buttons, SPEED / SPIN / RATE knobs and a READY LED (blinks while the wheels spin up, steady when ready, flickers per ball). The Pico 2 W also opens a Wi-Fi network PingPongThrower. Browse to 192.168.4.1 to get the same controls plus swing width and sweep period on your phone. A phone setting holds until you turn that knob again.

Bill of materials

Printed parts

Download the STL (any slicer) or the Bambu A1 project (0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layers, 10 % infill, settings included). P1S / X1C projects are in print/3mf/ (make 3mf). All parts print without supports.

Wiring

Everything goes on one 50×70 mm perfboard: the Pico 2 WH and the DRV8833 sit in female headers, with screw terminals for the battery and motors. The panel parts are wired to the board with short leads.
